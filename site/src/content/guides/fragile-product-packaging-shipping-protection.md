You just shipped 200 ceramic mugs to your warehouse, and 40 of them arrived in pieces. The outer boxes looked fine, but inside, the items had shifted and cracked against each other. Now you are facing a dead inventory pile, a costly reorder, and a delayed product launch.

The practical answer is that you need a layered approach: a rigid outer box, internal cushioning that absorbs shock, and a proper sealing method that holds the structure together under pressure. Good fragile product packaging prevents movement, absorbs impact, and keeps the product suspended away from the box walls.

Here is how to set up your box, cushioning, and sealing rules so your items arrive intact.

## Choose the Right Outer Box for Fragile Product Packaging
The outer box is your first line of defense. If the container fails, the cushioning inside will not matter. You need a box that can withstand vertical compression and rough handling.

### Understand Corrugated Board Strength
Shipping boxes are made of corrugated cardboard, which consists of a fluted sheet sandwiched between two flat liners. The most common ratings are single-wall and double-wall. Single-wall works for lighter items like small soaps or folded apparel. Double-wall, which has two fluted layers, works better for heavy glass jars or bulk ceramic orders.

### Leave Room for Cushioning
Your box needs to be larger than the product itself. A general rule is to leave 2 to 3 inches of space on all six sides of the item for cushioning material ([FedEx](https://www.fedex.com/en-us/shipping/packing.html)). If the box fits the product like a glove, there is no room for shock absorption. If the box is too large, the item will shift too far during sudden stops.

### Decide Between Mailer and Shipping Boxes
For single fragile items going directly to an ecommerce customer, a strong mailer box often works well. For bulk shipments or heavy items, a standard shipping box is necessary. You can compare the structural differences in our [mailer boxes vs. shipping boxes](/guides/mailer-boxes-vs-shipping-boxes/) guide.

## Select Cushioning for Fragile Products
Cushioning does two things: it absorbs impact and it fills empty space to prevent movement. The right material depends on the weight, shape, and fragility of your product.

### Compare Common Cushioning Materials
Different products require different types of interior protection. Here is a comparison of the most common options:

| Material | Best For | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air pillows | Filling voids in light shipments | Lightweight, clean | Can pop under heavy loads |
| Bubble wrap | Wrapping individual fragile items | Conforms to irregular shapes | Takes up storage space |
| Molded pulp | Electronics, glass bottles | Custom fit, rigid support | Requires custom tooling |
| Corrugated inserts | Dividing multiple items | High vertical strength | Adds weight to the package |
| Packing paper | Wrapping non-fragile edges | Inexpensive, recyclable | Compresses under heavy weight |

### Match the Insert to the Product
If you are shipping glass candles, you need inserts that grip the jar tightly. Corrugated inserts or molded pulp work well here because they hold the item in place. You can learn more about protecting glass items in our [glass jar candle packaging protection](/guides/glass-jar-candle-packaging-protection/) guide.

## Use Proper Box Sealing for Shipping
How you seal the box determines how well it holds together when stacked under other packages. A weak seal will burst open when the box is dropped on its corner.

### Choose the Right Tape
Use pressure-sensitive plastic tape, water-activated paper tape, or water-activated reinforced tape that is at least 2 to 3 inches wide ([USPS](https://www.usps.com/ship/packages.htm), [FedEx](https://www.fedex.com/en-us/shipping/packing.html)). Do not use masking tape, cellophane tape, or string. String and cord can get caught in automated sorting equipment, causing delays or damage.

### Apply the H-Taping Method
The H-taping method involves applying tape evenly across the center seam and both edge seams of the box flaps, forming an H shape ([FedEx](https://www.fedex.com/en-us/shipping/packing.html)). This secures the top and bottom of the box, preventing the flaps from catching on conveyor belts or popping open under pressure.

## Prevent Movement and Shock Damage
When a box is dropped, the contents inside experience the same sudden stop. If the product can move, it will build up momentum and strike the inside of the box.

### Block and Brace the Item
Blocking means using rigid pieces of corrugated board or foam to lock the product in place so it cannot shift in any direction. Bracing holds the item away from the box walls. This is critical for electronics or irregularly shaped ceramics.

### Test Your Package Before Shipping
Carriers recommend testing your package design before committing to a large shipment. Labs can simulate drop tests, vibration tests, and compression tests to find weak points. Learn how to prepare your custom packaging for [ISTA transit testing](/guides/ista-transit-testing-packaging-guide/) to verify your design will survive the supply chain.

## Label and Handle Fragile Package Protection
Labels communicate handling instructions to the carrier, but they do not replace physical protection. You still need to pack the box as if it will be dropped.

### Apply Shipping Labels Correctly
Place your shipping label on the largest, flattest surface of the box. Do not fold labels over edges or wrap them around corners, as scanners cannot read barcodes that bend. Make sure old shipping labels, barcodes, and addresses are completely covered or removed if you are reusing a box ([USPS](https://www.usps.com/ship/packages.htm)).

### Mark Orientation When Needed
If your product must stay upright, apply "This End Up" arrows or "Fragile" stickers. However, understand that carriers process thousands of packages an hour. Place the shipping label on the correct side to increase the chance the box stays oriented correctly ([FedEx](https://www.fedex.com/en-us/shipping/packing.html)).

## Common Mistakes to Avoid
Even experienced packaging buyers make errors when setting up fragile shipments. Watch out for these common pitfalls:

*   **Reusing weakened boxes:** Corrugated board loses its strength after a trip through the supply chain. If you reuse a box, ensure it has not been crushed, torn, or punctured ([FedEx](https://www.fedex.com/en-us/shipping/packing.html)).
*   **Using oversized boxes:** A box that is too large allows the product to shift, even if you fill the empty space with paper. The momentum of a shifting product can break through cushioning.
*   **Wrapping boxes in string:** Never wrap your box in string, cord, or twine. These materials get caught in USPS sorting equipment and can tear labels off the package ([USPS](https://www.usps.com/ship/packages.htm)).
*   **Underpaying postage:** If your package is large but light, dimensional weight pricing may apply. Always calculate postage accurately, including the weight of all packing materials, to avoid returns or delays ([USPS](https://www.usps.com/ship/packages.htm)).

## Purchasing and Specification Checklist
Before you [request a quote](/request-a-quote/) for fragile product packaging, gather these details to ensure your supplier understands your protection requirements.

*   **Product details:** Dimensions, weight, and fragility level.
*   **Box specifications:** Single-wall or double-wall, inner dimensions, and required burst strength.
*   **Insert requirements:** Material type, number of cavities, and item fit tolerance.
*   **Cushioning plan:** List of void fill materials needed for the master carton.
*   **Sealing rules:** Tape width and type, and H-taping instructions for packers.
*   **Master carton details:** Total master carton weight and dimensions for freight handoff.

## Conclusion
Effective fragile product packaging requires a rigid outer box, internal cushioning that absorbs shock, and proper sealing that holds the structure together. By leaving 2 to 3 inches of cushioning on all sides, using the H-taping method, and preventing internal movement, you can significantly reduce your damage rate during transit. Gather your product dimensions and insert requirements before you contact a supplier, and always test your design before committing to a large shipment.

## FAQ

### What is the best cushioning for fragile products?
Bubble wrap works well for wrapping individual irregular items, while molded pulp or corrugated inserts work best for holding multiple items in place. Air pillows are good for filling small voids but should not be the only protection for heavy items.

### How much space should be between the product and the box?
Leave 2 to 3 inches of space on all six sides of the product for cushioning material. This space absorbs impact and prevents the product from touching the box walls during a drop.

### Can I reuse boxes for shipping fragile items?
Yes, provided the box is sturdy and has not been crushed, torn, or otherwise damaged. However, corrugated board weakens with each use, so new boxes are recommended for high-value or heavy fragile items.

### What is the H-taping method?
The H-taping method involves applying packing tape across the center seam and both edge seams of the box flaps on the top and bottom. This creates an H shape and secures the box against bursting open.

### Does writing "Fragile" on the box guarantee careful handling?
No, carrier facilities process packages on automated belts, and handwritten notes do not override mechanical sorting. You must pack the item to survive drops and rough handling, treating labels as a secondary measure.
