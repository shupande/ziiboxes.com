The screen can display colors that physically cannot be recreated with standard printing inks. ## The Fundamental Reason for Packaging Color Difference

Color is not an absolute property of an object. It is a sensation produced when light hits an object, bounces off, and enters your eye. Because color depends on light, the way a screen creates color is fundamentally different from how ink creates color.

Your computer monitor or phone screen creates color by emitting light directly. It combines tiny red, green, and blue lights. When you look at a screen, you are looking at a light source. Packaging, however, relies on reflected light. You need an external light source—a lamp or the sun—to illuminate the paper and the inks so your eye can see the colors.

A color management system helps translate data from an RGB device (your screen) to a CMYK device (a printing press) so the printed result matches the original digital design as closely as possible. According to the [International Color Consortium](https://www.color.org/faqs.xalter), color management uses profiles to map colors from one device's gamut to another, aiming for a pleasing approximation when an exact match is physically impossible. Understanding this translation helps you see why a perfect screen-to-print match is limited.

## RGB vs CMYK Color Difference

Screens and printers use different color spaces. A color space defines the specific range of colors a device can produce. Your monitor uses the RGB color space, which has a very wide gamut—meaning it can display highly saturated, bright colors. Printing presses use the CMYK color space, which has a narrower gamut.

When you design packaging artwork in software like Photoshop or Illustrator, you might be working in RGB mode. If you send an RGB file to a printer, their software converts it to CMYK. During this conversion, bright neon greens, deep blues, and vivid oranges often shift to flatter, duller equivalents because the CMYK gamut cannot reproduce those wavelengths of light.

### Setting Up Your Files for Print

To avoid surprises, always design your [artwork file checklist](/guides/artwork-file-checklist/) in CMYK mode from the start. When you work in CMYK, your design software immediately restricts the color picker to shades that standard inks can actually reproduce. If a color looks dull on your screen while in CMYK mode, it will look similar on the printed box. [Pantone](https://www.pantone.com/eu/en-eu/articles/color-fundamentals/understanding-different-color-spaces) notes that RGB and CMYK represent entirely different color spaces, which explains why certain bright screen colors cannot be reproduced on a physical substrate like paperboard.

## How Paper Stock Changes Your Printed Colors

Ink behaves differently depending on the surface it touches. The same ink formulation will look noticeably different on coated versus uncoated paper. If you do not specify your paper type, you leave your packaging color difference to chance.

Coated paper has a shiny gloss coating. The ink sits on top of this coating, allowing for minimal ink absorption. This results in sharper, brighter, and more saturated colors. Uncoated paper has no surface coating, permitting maximum ink absorption into the paper fibers. The ink spreads slightly into the uncoated sheet, making colors appear flatter and softer.

According to [Pantone](https://support.pantone.com/en/what-does-the-terms-coated-and-uncoated-mean-in-the-pantone-graphics-system), the terms coated and uncoated refer to the paper finish, and the same Pantone color printed on coated versus uncoated paper will have a different visual appearance. The majority of this difference comes directly from the reaction of the ink to the paper, rather than the ink itself.

### Coated vs Uncoated Paper Effects

When you order [custom folding cartons](/products/custom-folding-cartons/), you will likely choose a coated white paperboard if you want bright, punchy colors. If you want a natural, muted aesthetic for items like handmade soap or organic food products, you might choose an uncoated kraft paper. Kraft paper is highly uncoated and absorbs a lot of ink, which significantly darkens and mutes standard CMYK colors. If you print a light yellow on kraft paper, it will look greenish or brown because the brown paper shows through.

## Pantone Packaging Color and Spot Inks

CMYK printing uses four transparent inks layered in tiny dots to simulate colors. This works well for photographs and most standard packaging designs. However, CMYK cannot reproduce certain highly saturated brand colors. If your brand relies on an exact, specific color, you need Pantone packaging color matching.

Pantone is a standardized color reproduction system. Instead of relying on layered CMYK dots, a printer mixes a specific recipe of inks to create a solid spot color. This ensures consistency across different print runs and materials. If you need a specific corporate blue, a Pantone spot ink guarantees that blue looks the same on every batch.

### When to Request a Spot Color

You should request a spot color when brand identity depends on a precise color match. You might also use spot colors for metallic inks, fluorescent inks, or very dark, rich blacks that standard CMYK cannot achieve. Keep in mind that adding a spot color often adds a printing plate and setup cost to your project. When looking at [printing finishes](/guides/printing-finishes/) for premium boxes, you will often see spot colors combined with foil stamping or spot UV for a high-end look.

## Other Factors That Widen the Color Gap

Beyond color spaces and paper types, several environmental and physical factors change how your packaging color difference appears to your eye.

**Screen Calibration**
Your monitor might not be calibrated for print work. Most consumer monitors are set to maximum brightness and high color saturation to make videos and photos pop. If you approve a dark blue on a bright monitor, the printed version will likely look much darker.

**Ambient Lighting**
The light in your office or home changes how you perceive color. Sunlight, fluorescent office lights, and warm incandescent bulbs all render color differently. Printers check colors under standardized daylight-balanced viewing booths to ensure consistency. You might be approving artwork under a warm desk lamp.

**Ink Film Thickness**
On a printing press, slight variances in ink film thickness can shift a color slightly lighter or darker. While modern presses maintain tight tolerances, physical ink application is never as flawless as a digital pixel.

**Lamination and Finishes**
Applying a matte lamination over your printed box will soften and slightly mute the colors underneath. A gloss lamination will deepen colors and add contrast, making them look wetter and more saturated than the raw print.

## Screen Color vs Print Color Comparison

Here is a practical comparison to help you understand how the different variables affect your final packaging.

| Factor | Screen (RGB) | Print (CMYK) |
|---|---|---|
| Light source | Emitted light from monitor | Reflected light from paper |
| Color gamut | Wide, highly saturated | Narrower, limited by ink chemistry |
| Substrate | Glass/LCD panel | Coated or uncoated paper |
| Brightness | Can be blindingly bright | Limited by ambient room light |
| Color consistency | Changes with screen settings | Changes with lighting and paper absorption |

## Purchasing and Specification Checklist

When you prepare to [request a quote](/request-a-quote/), give your supplier the right information to minimize color surprises. Here is what to include in your specification:

- Color Mode: Confirm your artwork is set to CMYK, not RGB.
- Pantone References: Provide specific Pantone codes for your brand colors (e.g., Pantone 185 C). Note whether the reference is coated or uncoated.
- Paper Type: Specify if you want coated white board or uncoated kraft.
- Finish: Note if you plan to use matte, gloss, or soft-touch lamination over the print.
- Physical Proof: Ask for a physical printed proof before mass production begins.
- Lighting Context: Review digital proofs in a well-lit room, not in the dark.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

Even experienced buyers make mistakes when managing packaging color difference. Avoid these common errors:

1. Approving colors on an uncalibrated laptop screen. Laptop screens shift color depending on your viewing angle. Look straight on, and try to use a desktop monitor for color approval.
2. Using RGB color values in a CMYK print file. The printer will convert the colors, and the shift will be unpredictable. Always convert to CMYK before finalizing your design.
3. Ignoring the paper type. If you print the same file on a glossy white box and a rough kraft box, you will get two completely different products.
4. Expecting a match to a hex code. Hex codes (like #FF0000) describe how a color looks on a screen. They do not dictate how ink dries on paper.
5. Skipping the physical proof. A digital proof on a screen cannot show you how the ink will look on the physical paper. Always request a physical proof for critical color matches.

## Making the Right Color Decision for Your Boxes

You will never get a 100% perfect match between a glowing screen and a physical paper box. The physics of light and ink simply do not allow it. ## FAQ

### Why does my packaging look darker than the design on my screen?

Screens emit light, making colors appear brighter and more saturated. Paper absorbs light, so printed colors naturally look darker. Your monitor brightness settings also exaggerate this difference.

### Should I send my artwork in RGB or CMYK format?

Always send artwork in CMYK format for printed packaging. If you send RGB files, the printer must convert them, which often causes unpredictable color shifts and a noticeable packaging color difference.

### What is a Pantone color and do I need it?

Pantone is a standardized spot color system that uses specific ink recipes rather than layered dots. You need it if your brand requires an exact, consistent color match across multiple print runs or different packaging materials.

### Does the paper type really change the ink color?

Yes. Coated paper keeps ink on the surface for bright, sharp colors. Uncoated paper absorbs ink into the fibers, making the same color look flatter and softer. The paper type is a major factor in the final color.

### How can I make sure my colors print correctly?

Design in CMYK mode, use Pantone references for critical brand colors, specify your paper stock clearly, and request a physical printed proof before approving mass production. Review your proofs in balanced lighting.
